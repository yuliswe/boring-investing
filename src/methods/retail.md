# Retail companies — analytical method

This method applies to companies whose primary business is selling physical
goods through company-operated stores and direct-to-consumer channels. It uses a
**franchise-quality** approach rather than a discounted-cash-flow model: the goal
is to assess whether the business has durable competitive advantages that sustain
high returns on capital, not to arrive at a precise intrinsic value.

## What we measure

### 1. Comparable-store sales growth

Same-store sales growth (often called "comp sales") isolates organic demand from
the effect of opening new locations. A retailer that grows revenue primarily
through comp sales is extracting more from its existing asset base, which is
higher-return growth than adding stores. Sustained comp growth above inflation
signals real brand pull rather than store-count arithmetic.

### 2. Store count and expansion runway

The number of company-operated stores at fiscal year-end, tracked over a decade,
reveals the pace and maturity of the physical expansion. A flattening store count
in a company's home market is not a problem if international markets are still
early, but it shifts the growth story to comp sales and channel mix.

### 3. Gross margin trend

Gross margin measures how much of every revenue dollar survives the cost of goods
sold — raw materials, manufacturing, freight, distribution, and store occupancy.
A rising gross margin at scale indicates pricing power and supply-chain
efficiency; a falling one may signal discounting pressure or input-cost inflation
that the brand cannot pass through.

### 4. Inventory turnover

Cost of goods sold divided by average inventory. Higher turnover means the
company is selling through merchandise faster and holding less capital in unsold
stock. A declining trend can signal overbuying, stale product, or demand
softening before it shows up in the income statement.

### 5. Operating margin trend

A widening margin on growing revenue signals operating leverage — the company is
growing revenue faster than it adds overhead. Retailers typically operate on
thinner margins than software companies, so even a few percentage points of
margin expansion over a decade is meaningful.

### 6. Free cash flow conversion

High free cash flow relative to net income confirms that reported earnings
translate into cash. Retailers that own stores carry significant capital
expenditure for new builds and maintenance, so free cash flow often lags net
income more than it does for asset-light businesses.

## What we do not do

- **No DCF or target price.** The page presents data and a qualitative thesis,
  not a valuation or a buy/sell recommendation.
- **No quarterly tracking.** Figures are fiscal-year snapshots. Quarterly noise
  is omitted deliberately.
- **No peer comparison.** Each company is assessed on its own merits.
  Cross-company comparison belongs to a screen, not a company page.
