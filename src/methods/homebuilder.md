# Homebuilders — analytical method

This method applies to companies that buy land, develop lots and build homes for
sale. It anchors valuation on the balance sheet rather than on earnings, because
a builder's earnings swing with the housing cycle far more than its assets do.
Price-to-earnings is unreliable in both directions, since it looks cheapest at a
cyclical peak when margins are about to fall and most expensive at a trough when
earnings are temporarily depressed or negative.

## What we measure

### 1. Price-to-tangible-book (P/TBV)

P/TBV is the anchor. A builder's balance sheet is mostly land and homes under
construction, so tangible book value (stockholders' equity minus goodwill and
other intangible assets) is a rough floor on what the business is worth.
Goodwill is excluded because it records a premium paid for another builder,
which cannot be sold separately from the land it came with.

Historically, builders bottom near or below 1x tangible book and peak at 2–3x.
Each page compares the company's current multiple with its own history and with
peers such as D.R. Horton, PulteGroup and NVR.

### 2. Return on equity across a full cycle

P/TBV only makes sense alongside return on equity (ROE). A builder earning
15–20% on equity deserves to trade well above book, and one earning 5% does not.
The question is what ROE the company earns across a full cycle, including the
2006–2011 housing bust, rather than in any single year, because a peak-year ROE
paired with a trough-year multiple flatters the stock.

ROE is net income attributable to the company divided by the average of
beginning and ending stockholders' equity. A full-cycle figure averages ROE over
every year from fiscal 2006 onward.

### 3. Pairing the two

The two metrics are read together. A multiple of book is justified by the
spread between ROE and the cost of equity, so a low P/TBV is only cheap if the
company can earn its full-cycle ROE again, and a high P/TBV is only expensive if
the current ROE is above what the cycle will sustain. Peer comparisons plot each
builder's current P/TBV against its full-cycle ROE for that reason.

## What we still show

The standard sections (revenue, cost analysis, cash flow and filings) still
apply. Cash flow from operations for a builder moves inversely with land
spending, so it runs negative while the company grows its land book and turns
strongly positive when it slows down, and it should not be read as a
profitability measure in isolation.
