# Software companies — analytical method

This method applies to companies whose primary business is selling software,
whether as licences, subscriptions, or cloud services. It uses a
**franchise-quality** approach rather than a discounted-cash-flow model: the goal
is to assess whether the business has durable competitive advantages that sustain
high returns on capital, not to arrive at a precise intrinsic value.

## What we measure

### 1. Revenue growth by segment

Track each reporting segment separately so that a slowdown in one segment does
not hide behind strength in another. For multi-product software companies the
split typically falls along product lines (cloud, productivity, devices); for
single-product companies a geographic or customer-size split may be more
revealing.

### 2. Operating margin trend

A widening margin on growing revenue signals pricing power and operating
leverage — both hallmarks of a franchise. A margin that compresses while revenue
grows points to commoditisation or investment-heavy expansion whose returns are
unproven. Software companies in particular should show strong incremental margins
because the marginal cost of distributing software is near zero.

### 3. Free cash flow conversion

High free cash flow relative to net income confirms that reported earnings
translate into cash the company can return or reinvest. Persistent gaps between
the two warrant investigation. Common culprits in software companies are large
stock-based compensation (which depresses cash flow less than it depresses
earnings) and capitalised development costs.

### 4. Recurring revenue share

The fraction of revenue that is contractually recurring (subscriptions, support
contracts, consumption-based cloud) versus one-time (licences, hardware, services)
determines how predictable the revenue base is and how resilient it would be in a
downturn.

### 5. Capital allocation

Buybacks, dividends, and acquisition spending are noted qualitatively. The
question is whether management reinvests at returns above the cost of capital or
distributes excess cash, not whether one policy is inherently better than the
other.

## What we do not do

- **No DCF or target price.** The page presents data and a qualitative thesis,
  not a valuation or a buy/sell recommendation.
- **No quarterly tracking.** Figures are fiscal-year snapshots. Quarterly noise
  is omitted deliberately.
- **No peer comparison.** Each company is assessed on its own merits.
  Cross-company comparison belongs to a screen, not a company page.
