# Oil and gas companies — analytical method

This method applies to companies that produce crude oil, bitumen or natural gas,
including integrated companies that also refine and market what they produce.
It takes a **cost-position and capital-discipline** approach rather than a
growth approach. These companies sell a commodity whose price they do not set,
so the goal is to judge whether the business earns acceptable returns at a
mid-cycle oil price, and whether the share price assumes that today's price will
last.

## Why the usual metrics break

- **Earnings follow the oil price.** Revenue, margins and EPS move with WTI and
  refining cracks far more than with anything management does. A low P/E at a
  price peak is not cheap, and a high or negative P/E at a trough is not
  expensive. Every year's figures are read beside the average oil price for that
  year.
- **Revenue growth says little.** Higher revenue usually means a higher oil
  price, not a bigger or better business. Production volume and cost per barrel
  are the measures of operating progress.
- **Depletion is a real cost.** Reserves run down as they are produced, so part
  of operating cash flow must be reinvested just to hold production flat.
  Capital spending is split into sustaining and growth spending where the
  company reports the split.
- **Working capital swings with prices.** Receivables, payables and inventory
  move sharply when prices move, so operating cash flow before working capital
  changes (often reported as funds from operations) is the better base for
  free cash flow.

The metrics are grouped into four sections, in the order a reader should take
them.

## 1. Valuation

| Metric                     | Calculation                                                         | What to watch for                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| P/E ratio                  | Year-end price ÷ diluted EPS                                        | Read it against that year's oil price. The ratio is lowest at price peaks, when earnings are about to fall.               |
| Price to free funds flow   | Year-end price ÷ (funds from operations − capital spending) / share | Strips working capital swings from free cash flow. Compare it at similar oil prices rather than across the whole history. |
| EV / funds from operations | (Market capitalization + net debt) ÷ funds from operations          | Includes debt, so a company that has paid down debt looks cheaper on this measure than on P/E alone.                      |

## 2. Cost position

| Metric                    | Calculation                                                                                                 | What to watch for                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cash operating cost / bbl | Production costs ÷ barrels produced, as reported                                                            | The best measure of competitive position. A falling cost per barrel at flat or rising volume is real operating progress, independent of the oil price. |
| Production                | Barrels per day, as reported                                                                                | Growth from existing assets (debottlenecking, reliability) is cheaper than growth from new projects. Asset sales change the base.                      |
| Refinery utilization      | Crude processed ÷ nameplate capacity, as reported                                                           | For integrated companies, high utilization captures the refining margin on the company's own crude.                                                    |
| WTI breakeven             | Oil price at which funds from operations cover sustaining capital and the dividend, as stated by management | The lower it is, the more of a price fall the company can absorb before it must borrow or cut the dividend.                                            |

## 3. Capital and balance sheet

| Metric                     | Calculation                                               | What to watch for                                                                                                                            |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Return on equity           | Net income ÷ average shareholders' equity                 | Average it across the cycle. Impairments in a price crash drag single years far below the norm.                                              |
| Net debt / funds from ops. | (Debt + lease liabilities − cash) ÷ funds from operations | Below 1× leaves room to keep paying the dividend through a price crash. Above 2× in a normal year is a warning.                              |
| Shareholder payout         | (Dividends + buybacks) ÷ free funds flow                  | Above 100% means returns are funded by debt or asset sales. Buybacks that rise with the oil price buy the most shares at the highest prices. |
| Diluted share count        | Diluted weighted average shares                           | A falling count lifts per-share production and cash flow even when the company does not grow.                                                |

## 4. Price sensitivity

Each page shows the company's own published sensitivities of funds from
operations to a US$1/bbl change in WTI, a change in refining cracks, the
exchange rate and natural gas prices, converted to a per-share amount. These
show how much of the current valuation depends on the current oil price.

## What we do not do

- **No oil price forecast.** The page shows what has happened at past prices
  and what the current price implies. It does not predict the oil price.
- **No target price.** The reverse DCF shows what the share price requires, and
  the page does not present a valuation or a buy/sell recommendation.
- **No quarterly tracking.** Figures are fiscal-year snapshots, and quarterly
  noise is left out on purpose.
- **No peer comparison.** Each company is assessed on its own merits.
  Cross-company comparison belongs to a screen, not to a company page.
